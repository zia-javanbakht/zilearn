Since mass is conserved, $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}M_{S_p} = 0$, so the closed-system identity (3) applies with $\phi = \boldsymbol{v}$ (so $\dot{\boldsymbol{v}} = \boldsymbol{a}$). Using Cauchy's theorem (1) to write $\boldsymbol{t} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$, and the divergence theorem (2):

$$\int_{S_p} \rho\boldsymbol{a}\,\mathrm{d}v = \int_{S_p} \rho\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{d}v + \int_{S_p} \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int_{S_p} (\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma} + \rho\boldsymbol{b} - \rho\boldsymbol{a})\,\mathrm{d}v = 0.$$

The cut-out $S_p$ is arbitrary, so the localisation theorem (Theorem 2.3) gives the local form of the balance of linear momentum:

$$\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma} + \rho\boldsymbol{b} = \rho\boldsymbol{a}.$$

## 5 Conservation of Angular Momentum

**Definition 5.1 — Angular Momentum Balance.** The angular momentum of the cut-out $S_p$ is $\boldsymbol{H}_{S_p} = \int_{S_p} \boldsymbol{x}\times\rho\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}v$. Its rate of change equals the resultant moment generated by the body and surface forces acting on $S_p$:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{S_p} \boldsymbol{x}\times\rho\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}v = \int_{S_p} \boldsymbol{x}\times\rho\boldsymbol{b}\,\mathrm{d}v + \int_{\partial S_p} \boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{t}\,\mathrm{d}a.$$

The closed-system identity (3) applied to $\phi = \boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{v}$ gives $\dot{\phi} = \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{v} + \boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{a} = \boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{a}$, so the left side of (11) becomes $\int_{S_p} \boldsymbol{x}\times\rho\boldsymbol{a}\,\mathrm{d}v$. Writing the surface term in index notation with $t_k = \sigma_{kl}n_l$ and applying the divergence theorem,

$$\int_{\partial S_p} \varepsilon_{ijk}x_j\sigma_{kl}n_l\,\mathrm{d}a = \int_{S_p} \varepsilon_{ijk}\frac{\partial(x_j\sigma_{kl})}{\partial x_l}\,\mathrm{d}v = \int_{S_p} \varepsilon_{ijk}\big(\sigma_{kj} + x_j(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})_k\big)\,\mathrm{d}v.$$
